5.1 · Software Quality

Quality, quality factors and metrics

Software quality is the degree to which a software product meets its stated requirements and satisfies its intended users in the conditions where they use it. Conformance to requirements (Crosby) asks whether the software matches the specification. Fitness for use (Juran) asks whether it serves the user’s actual purpose. Quality is multidimensional. A product may be functionally correct and still be slow, difficult to use, unreliable, or expensive to maintain.

AspectWhat is evaluatedSoftware example
Product qualityThe properties of the delivered software and other work products.The banking app calculates the correct balance, loads quickly and lets users complete a transfer.
Process qualityHow consistently the team uses effective practices throughout development and maintenance.Requirements are reviewed, code changes are checked, and the team tracks defects through closure.

Process quality influences product quality. For example, reviewing the requirements before implementation can catch an omitted transfer limit that would otherwise appear as a defect during system testing or after release. Teams still measure the delivered product to verify the outcome.

Five views of software quality

Garvin’s five views explain why stakeholders can judge the same software differently. Each view asks a different question about the product.

ViewMeaning and evidenceBanking application example
Transcendent or transcendentalQuality is perceived excellence. It is recognised through experience and is difficult to capture in a single objective measure.The interface feels coherent, polished and dependable when a user tries it.
User-basedQuality is fitness for use. Evidence includes task completion, usability observations and user satisfaction.Customers can find and complete a money transfer without help.
Manufacturing-basedQuality is conformance to specifications. Reviews, standards and verification tests provide evidence.The transfer limit and rejection message match the approved requirements.
Product-basedQuality is assessed through measurable attributes of the product.Release A has lower defect density and shorter response time than release B under the same test conditions.
Value-basedQuality is the balance between the benefits delivered and the total cost of acquiring, operating and maintaining the product.A bank selects a solution that meets its essential needs at an acceptable lifecycle cost.

Past-paper application. A banking app’s functions work correctly, but customers find it difficult to navigate. The user-based view is most affected because users struggle to achieve their goals. In McCall’s model, the related factor is usability under product operation. Check task completion, time taken and navigation errors with representative users.

McCall’s quality factors and criteria

McCall’s model groups 11 software quality factors into three perspectives. A factor describes a desirable property. A criterion is a property used to assess that factor. A metric measures or scores the criterion. For example, correctness is a factor, traceability is a criterion, and the percentage of requirements linked to verified tests is one possible metric.

PerspectiveFactorMeaningAssociated criteria
OperationCorrectnessDegree to which the software satisfies its specifications and objectives.Completeness, consistency, traceability
OperationReliabilityAbility to perform its intended functions without failure under stated conditions.Accuracy, consistency, error tolerance
OperationEfficiencyTime, storage and other computing resources required to perform a function.Execution efficiency, storage efficiency
OperationIntegrityAbility to protect software and data from unauthorised access.Access control, access audit
OperationUsabilityEffort needed to learn, operate and interpret the software.Operability, training, communicativeness
RevisionMaintainabilityEffort required to locate and correct a defect.Simplicity, conciseness, modularity, self-descriptiveness
RevisionFlexibilityEffort needed to modify software for a new requirement.Expandability, generality, modularity, self-descriptiveness
RevisionTestabilityEffort required to exercise the software and verify its behaviour.Instrumentation, simplicity
TransitionPortabilityEffort required to move software to a different platform or environment.Machine independence, software-system independence, modularity, self-descriptiveness
TransitionReusabilityExtent to which a component can be used in another application.Generality, modularity, self-descriptiveness, machine and software-system independence
TransitionInteroperabilityAbility to exchange and use information across systems.Communication commonality, data commonality

How criteria are applied. Traceability links requirements to corresponding work products. Error tolerance concerns continued operation or orderly recovery when errors occur. Instrumentation makes behaviour observable through logs, probes or diagnostic outputs. Machine independence limits dependence on specific hardware. Data and communication commonality describe the use of compatible formats and protocols.

Use and limitations. The classification gives teams a shared vocabulary and helps turn quality expectations into checks. Criteria can support several factors, and priorities depend on the system. A banking system may emphasise integrity and reliability, while a reusable library may place extra weight on portability and reusability. Document the scoring method if combining multiple measures.

10-mark structure. Define the model and distinguish factor, criterion and metric. Draw the three branches, explain all 11 factors, then link representative criteria and a software example to the factors.

Software quality metrics

A software quality metric is a quantitative measure of a quality attribute of a product, process or maintenance activity. Define the unit, data source, time window, counting rules and target before comparing results. The textbook groups lifecycle quality measures into end-product quality, in-process quality and maintenance metrics. Project measures such as staffing and schedule provide context.

End-product quality metrics

MetricMeaning and calculationWorked example
Mean time to failure (MTTF)Average time to failure. A simple test estimate is total operating time accumulated across the observations divided by observed failures.600 operating hours ÷ 3 failures = 200 hours per failure in the observation.
Defect densityCount of confirmed defects ÷ software size, commonly measured in KLOC (thousand lines of code) or function points.18 defects ÷ 6 KLOC = 3 defects/KLOC.
Problems per user month (PUM)Customer-reported problems ÷ licensed user-months in the same period. Reports may include usability or documentation problems.60 reports over one month with 300 continuously installed licences = 0.20 problems per user-month.
Customer satisfactionFor a five-point survey, one measure is (satisfied + very satisfied responses) ÷ total valid responses × 100.84 positive responses out of 100 = 84% satisfied.

In-process quality metrics

  • Defect density during testing. Confirmed defects found in testing per size unit. Compare releases with similar test scope and effort. A high value may reflect more defects, better detection, or both.
  • Defect-arrival pattern. Plot new defects by day or week. A sustained drop with comparable test effort can indicate stabilisation. A drop caused by fewer tests or blocked environments warrants investigation.
  • Defect removal efficiency (DRE). A release-level measure is pre-release defects removed ÷ (pre-release defects removed + escaped defects discovered in a stated post-release window) × 100. If 90 are removed before release and 10 emerge within 30 days, DRE is 90%. Compare releases using equivalent windows and counting rules.

Textbook notation for monthly flow in §13.10 uses defects removed during the month ÷ problem arrivals during that month × 100, also labelled DRE there. A monthly closure ratio can exceed 100% if older items are closed. Use the formula that matches the question’s data and state the denominator.

Software maintenance metrics

MetricCalculation or interpretation
Fix backlogCount of unresolved issues at the end of a period. Ending backlog = opening backlog + arrivals − closures, assuming no other status adjustments.
Backlog management index (BMI)Issues closed ÷ new issues received in the period × 100. Above 100% indicates shrinking backlog under the same counting rules. At 100%, backlog is unchanged. Below 100%, it grows. With zero arrivals the ratio is undefined.
Fix response timeAverage time from opening to closure for the chosen severity group. Compare observed turnaround against the agreed fix time to assess responsiveness.
Percent delinquent fixesFixes that miss the agreed response-time criterion ÷ fixes delivered in the stated period and severity group × 100.
Percent defective fixesFixes that fail to resolve the defect or introduce a new one ÷ total fixes evaluated × 100.

Worked BMI. A team starts with 25 open issues, receives 20 and closes 16. Ending backlog = 25 + 20 − 16 = 29. BMI = 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80%. The backlog grew by four because arrivals exceeded closures.

10-mark structure. Define software quality metrics, explain the three lifecycle groups and give formulas with units and interpretation. For “any four product quality metrics”, use the four end-product rows and one numeric example for each.

5.2 · Quality Management

Plan, assure, control and improve

Software quality management (SQM) is the coordinated work of setting quality objectives, planning how to achieve them, assuring that suitable processes are followed, checking work products, and improving the processes that produce them. Management supplies resources and resolves escalation. Developers, testers, and other stakeholders contribute evidence and corrective actions.

PDCA cycle

Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) is a repeating cycle for testing and sustaining a process improvement. The next cycle uses what the team learns from the current one.

StageWhat to doWorked example of flaky tests
PlanDefine the problem, baseline, target and proposed change. Decide how to measure the result.Eight of 100 scheduled CI runs have failures that disappear without a code change. Target at most three per 100 comparable runs. Identify shared test data as a possible cause.
DoPilot the change on a limited scale. Record what was implemented and collect evidence.Give each run isolated test data for the affected test suite. Track failures for the next 100 comparable runs.
CheckCompare results against the baseline and target. Look for other changes that could explain the result.Two of 100 runs are flaky. Confirm that test coverage and environment conditions remained comparable.
ActStandardise a successful change, assign monitoring and apply it more widely. If results fall short, revise the plan and repeat.Make isolated data the default for this suite and track the rate in future releases.

Software quality plan

A quality plan states the project’s quality objectives and specifies the standards, activities, responsibilities, resources and evidence used to achieve and demonstrate them. It is prepared early, agreed with stakeholders, and maintained as scope, requirements or risks change. A test plan describes the testing portion in more detail, including test scope, approach, environments, resources and schedule.

Plan componentWhat to writeBanking app example
Scope and referencesProduct, release, deliverables, applicable requirements and standards.Login and transfer release. Reference the approved requirements and review procedure.
Objectives and acceptance criteriaMeasurable quality targets, conditions and release gates.No open critical defects. All mandatory transfer acceptance tests pass.
Roles and resourcesOwners, training, escalation, tools, environments and data.QA lead owns evidence. Developers own fixes. Product owner approves any release exception.
QA and QC activitiesReviews, audits, test levels, deliverable checks, milestones and approval points.Review the requirements early, audit traceability and run system and acceptance tests before release.
Measurement and reportingMetric definitions, data sources, targets, collection frequency and review cadence.Review open defects by severity and retest status at each release checkpoint.
Defect and change managementRecording, triage, corrective action, retest, configuration control and escalation.Record defects with evidence and verify fixes against the exact release build.
Risk, records and approvalQuality risks, contingencies, retained records and sign-off responsibilities.Use a payment sandbox if external test access is delayed. Retain test results and approval decisions.

Benefits. A quality plan gives people clear ownership, establishes consistent checks, helps surface risks early, and makes the release decision traceable to evidence.

Quality assurance, quality control and methods

ActivityMain questionExamples and outputs
Quality planningWhat quality should we achieve, and how will we show it?Set objectives, standards, responsibilities, acceptance criteria and measures. Output is a quality plan.
Quality assurance (QA)Do the processes and standards provide confidence in the quality of the work?Audit review records, verify change-control compliance and report nonconformities. Outputs include audit findings and corrective-action records.
Quality control (QC)Does this work product meet the specified quality requirements?Inspect requirements, run tests, log defects and retest fixes. Outputs include inspection or test results and defect records.

Software quality assurance (SQA) is the planned, systematic evaluation of software processes and work products against applicable standards. An SQA audit can inspect sample evidence, document deviations, agree action owners and dates, and verify that the findings were addressed. SQA includes both process monitoring (were the prescribed steps followed?) and product evaluation against standards (does the work product meet the required documentation or coding standard?).

QC finds and helps remove defects by comparing intermediate and final outputs with measurable specifications. It includes static checks, such as requirements and code inspections, and dynamic checks, such as functional, performance and acceptance testing. A review used to find errors in an SRS is QC. Auditing whether teams carry out the required SRS reviews is QA.

Two quality management methods from the reference book
ApproachHow it worksExample
ProceduralDefine standards and procedures for verification, validation, reviews, inspections, tests and audits. Engineers perform V&V and SQA reviews conformance.Review and approve each transfer requirement. Execute the linked acceptance tests.
QuantitativeSet goals, collect consistent measurements through a software metrics programme, compare results to targets and use the findings to improve.Track escaped defects per release, investigate an increase, and measure results after the process change.

Quality cost and benefits

Cost of quality (COQ) is the sum of what the organisation spends to achieve and evaluate quality and what it loses because quality requirements are missed.

COQ = prevention + appraisal + internal failure + external failure
CategoryPurpose or timingSoftware examples
PreventionReduce the likelihood of introducing defects.Quality planning, training, defining coding standards and improving a recurring defect-prone process.
AppraisalEvaluate whether work products meet expectations.Planned testing, inspections, verification and measurement activities.
Internal failureCorrect or handle defects discovered before delivery.Debugging a system-test defect, rework, rebuilding and retesting the fix.
External failureHandle defects discovered after delivery.Customer support, emergency patches, rollback, downtime, refunds or penalties.

Prevention and appraisal are commonly grouped as the cost of conformance. Internal and external failures form the cost of nonconformance or cost of poor quality in this four-category breakdown. Classify an activity by its purpose. Scheduled release testing is appraisal. Extra regression testing needed to verify a pre-release fix is an internal failure cost.

Worked comparison. Before a change, prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure cost ₹10k, ₹15k, ₹30k and ₹45k respectively. COQ = ₹100k. After investing more in early reviews and testing, those costs are ₹18k, ₹20k, ₹20k and ₹22k. COQ = ₹80k. An extra ₹13k in conformance spending accompanies a ₹33k reduction in failure costs, giving a ₹20k net reduction in this illustration.

Benefits of investing in quality. Earlier prevention and detection can reduce rework, customer incidents and support costs. Less repeated debugging improves productivity and schedule predictability. Fewer defects and better usability improve customer satisfaction. The investment should be evaluated using comparable periods, severity and business impact.

Defect prevention and root cause analysis

Defect prevention reduces the chance of defects being introduced or recurring. It includes clarifying requirements with examples, training, reusable design standards, reviews, and prevention actions drawn from defect history. Root cause analysis (RCA) is a systematic investigation of the underlying causes and conditions that produced a problem or allowed it to escape.

  1. Define the problem. Record the observed behaviour, impact, scope, affected versions and when it occurs.
  2. Collect and compare evidence. Use defect reports, logs, timestamps, build or configuration history and controlled reproduction. Compare failed and successful interactions.
  3. Generate candidate causes. Use an Ishikawa diagram to group possibilities and the 5 Whys to explore a plausible chain. Several contributing causes may exist.
  4. Verify the cause. Check the evidence, reproduce the conditions and test whether removing the cause prevents the failure.
  5. Assign and implement actions. Correct the immediate issue, address the underlying process cause and specify owners and due dates.
  6. Verify effectiveness. Retest the fix, run appropriate regression checks and monitor recurrence or the relevant defect rate.

Past-paper scenario. Many users report login failures during system testing of a banking app. Determine whether failures cluster by account, build, time, browser or application server. Candidate causes include bad test data, a configuration mismatch, overloaded authentication services, or unclear error messages. Treat each cause as a hypothesis.

Illustrative evidence and 5 Whys. Suppose logs show that authentication requests fail only on instances with an outdated identity-service configuration. Why were logins failing? Requests reached an outdated endpoint. Why was that endpoint used? A stale configuration remained on part of the deployment. Why did it remain? The deployment process permitted different settings across instances. Why was that possible? Configuration consistency was checked manually. Why did the defect escape? The release checks lacked an automated comparison and a login smoke test across instances.

Actions and verification. Correct the stale settings, validate configurations automatically during deployment and run login smoke tests on each instance. Reproduce the previous conditions, verify successful login and track the failure rate across subsequent comparable builds.

10-mark structure. For a quality-management question, use the quality plan and QA/QC comparison, then explain procedural and quantitative methods. For an RCA scenario, define the symptom, show candidate causes, describe evidence that would confirm a cause, and give prevention actions and verification.

5.3 · Software Quality Tools

Ishikawa, checklist, control chart and Pareto chart

ToolQuestion it answersWhat it produces
Ishikawa diagramWhat could be causing this problem?Grouped hypotheses for investigation.
ChecklistDid we verify every required item?Completion status, evidence and follow-up items.
Control chartIs this process statistically stable over time?A time-ordered chart with a centre line and control limits.
Pareto chartWhich categories contribute most to the problem?Ranked bars with a cumulative percentage line.

Ishikawa diagram (fishbone or cause-and-effect diagram)

An Ishikawa diagram puts the observed effect at the head of a horizontal spine and branches potential causes into categories. Its purpose is to organise investigation and help the team look beyond the first explanation.

  1. Write a specific effect at the head. Agree on the affected group, build or time period.
  2. Draw the spine and main bones. The classic 6M headings are people (manpower), methods, machines, materials, measurement and environment. Adapt the wording to the software context.
  3. Brainstorm possible causes, attach them to the relevant bones and add sub-causes where needed.
  4. Prioritise hypotheses using available data. Verify plausible causes through logs, comparisons or controlled tests.

Significance. The diagram gives the team a shared picture of possible causes, including process and environment issues. The drawing alone does not establish causation. In the banking example, comparing configuration versions on failed and successful instances can test the stale-configuration hypothesis.

Checklist

A quality checklist is a list of predefined items that must be checked during an inspection, review, test or release decision. Each item should be observable and specific, with space for its result and evidence.

Sample banking release checklist
Check itemStatusEvidence or follow-up
Every mandatory requirement has an acceptance test.YesApproved traceability matrix.
Production-like configuration comparison passed.NoQA lead to repeat after the configuration correction.
Critical defects are closed and retested.YesDefect register and verified retest report.
Data migration was rehearsed on a safe test copy.N/ANo schema or data migration in this release.

How to use it. Build items from requirements, standards, risks and known defects. Record Yes, No or N/A with evidence. Assign unresolved items and recheck closure. Checklists improve consistency and reduce omissions. Keep room for exploratory checks and update the list when risks change.

Checklist and check sheet. A checklist verifies completion or conformance. A check sheet records how often events occur, such as daily counts of authentication, network and validation defects. Check-sheet counts can become the input for a Pareto chart.

Control chart

A control chart displays a statistic over time to assess process stability. The centre line (CL) represents the baseline process level. The upper and lower control limits (UCL and LCL) represent expected variation under the chosen chart model. Select the chart based on the data and sampling method. A p-chart, for example, tracks proportions of nonconforming units.

  1. Choose an operational metric and collect time-ordered, comparable samples. Record sample size and conditions.
  2. Establish the centre line and appropriate control limits using a stable baseline. Many Shewhart charts use limits three standard errors of the plotted statistic from the centre line.
  3. Plot each observation in order. Investigate points outside the limits and unusual runs or trends inside them.
  4. Look for the cause of any signal. Document the action, then monitor whether performance stabilises.

Worked p-chart. From a stable baseline, 100 of 2,000 login test attempts fail, so the baseline fraction is 0.05. For each later batch of 100 comparable attempts, standard error = √(0.05 × 0.95 ÷ 100) ≈ 0.0218. UCL = 0.05 + 3 × 0.0218 ≈ 11.54%. LCL is truncated to 0% because the calculated lower limit is negative. A later batch with 14 failures out of 100 exceeds the UCL and warrants investigation.

Interpretation. Common-cause variation is the routine variation of the process. A special-cause signal suggests that something changed. Control limits describe process behaviour and come from process data. Specification limits are acceptance requirements established by customers or engineering. A stable process can still fail to meet its required target.

Pareto chart

A Pareto chart orders problem categories from largest to smallest and often overlays a line for cumulative percentage. It helps direct improvement effort at the categories with the largest measured contribution. The “80/20 rule” is a rule of thumb, and the actual distribution comes from the data.

Defects grouped by primary category in one release
CategoryCountIndividual shareCumulative share
Authentication4040%40%
Validation3030%70%
Performance1515%85%
UI1010%95%
Other55%100%
  1. Define categories and a measurement window. Use one primary category per defect for this example.
  2. Count defects in each category and sort the categories in descending order.
  3. Calculate each cumulative percentage as running total ÷ grand total × 100.
  4. Draw bars using the left count axis and the cumulative line using the right percentage axis. Identify priorities and remeasure after taking action.

Authentication and validation account for 70% of all defects. Adding performance reaches 85%. Use severity or cost as a separate prioritisation lens when a rare problem has significant impact.

5.4 · SQA Models and Process Improvement

Standards, STQM, Six Sigma and TMM

SQA models and standards

SQA models and standards give an organisation common criteria for specifying product quality, governing processes, or assessing maturity. The slides and book discuss ISO/IEC 9126, the ISO 9000 family and the Capability Maturity Model (CMM). Each answers a different question.

Model or standardFocusTypical evidence
ISO/IEC 9126Which characteristics describe the quality of a software product?Quality requirements, metric results and rating criteria.
ISO 9000 familyHow does the organisation manage quality consistently?Quality objectives, process records, audits and improvement actions.
Software CMMHow mature is the organisation’s software development process?Evidence of project controls, standard processes, measurement and sustained improvement.
TMMHow mature is the organisation’s testing process?Test policies, training, lifecycle integration, metrics and defect-prevention records.

ISO/IEC 9126 software product quality model

Use the historical model taught in the slides. It defines six quality characteristics and helps customers and developers agree on what to evaluate.

CharacteristicWhat to evaluate
FunctionalityWhether suitable functions provide the expected results.
ReliabilityWhether performance is maintained under specified conditions, including recovery after failure.
UsabilityHow readily users understand, learn and operate the product.
EfficiencyTime behaviour and resource usage under stated conditions.
MaintainabilityEffort to analyse, change and test the product after modification.
PortabilityEase of adapting or installing the software in another environment.

Evaluation sequence. Define quality requirements, prepare the evaluation by selecting measures and rating criteria, then execute the evaluation and make a decision. For efficiency, a testable requirement could set the 95th-percentile response time to at most two seconds at 100 concurrent users. Test under those conditions and compare the result against the stated limit.

ISO 9000 family and the process approach

The ISO 9000 family addresses the organisation’s quality management system (QMS). ISO 9000 covers fundamentals and vocabulary, while ISO 9001 sets QMS requirements. The lecture’s 2000 revision highlights the shift toward managing interconnected processes. Customer requirements feed product and service delivery. Management responsibility and resource management support delivery. Measurement, analysis and improvement use the outcomes and customer feedback to improve the QMS.

Benefits and effort. The process approach helps assign ownership, document how work is performed and produce audit evidence. Introducing and maintaining it requires time for training, records and process changes. A QMS assessment evaluates how the organisation manages quality. Software acceptance still depends on product evidence.

Software Capability Maturity Model (CMM)

The Software Engineering Institute’s Software CMM describes progressive maturity of the software development process. Levels contain key process areas (KPAs). Common features organise the key practices that help an organisation achieve KPA goals and institutionalise the process.

LevelSoftware CMM meaning
1 · InitialAd hoc processes depend heavily on individual effort.
2 · RepeatableBasic project management makes earlier results repeatable on similar projects.
3 · DefinedThe organisation documents standard processes and tailors them for projects.
4 · ManagedProcess and product quality are measured and managed quantitatively.
5 · OptimizingFeedback and innovations drive continuous process improvement.

10-mark structure. Define the purpose of an SQA model. Explain ISO/IEC 9126 and its six attributes, describe the ISO 9000 process approach, then list the five CMM levels and state what each model evaluates.

Software Total Quality Management (STQM)

STQM applies Total Quality Management to a software organisation. It is a customer-focused, organisation-wide approach in which senior management supports every team in improving software products, processes and services through evidence and continuous improvement.

ElementMeaningSoftware application
Customer focusUnderstand customer needs and measure whether the product meets them.Observe users trying to complete a bank transfer and identify steps where they abandon it.
Process focusImprove the development and business processes that shape the outcome.Review acceptance criteria with users before coding and improve the design review process.
Human side of qualityBuild management commitment, employee participation, training and ownership.Give developers, testers, designers and support staff time to analyse the problem together.
Measurement and analysisUse goals, metrics and feedback to guide decisions and confirm improvements.Compare transfer completion, errors and support requests before and after redesign.

Implementation. Set customer-oriented quality goals, identify the processes that shape them, gather a baseline, train and involve teams, apply improvements through PDCA, and standardise changes that demonstrate a benefit. SQA gives evidence that agreed practices are applied. STQM extends responsibility for improving those practices across the organisation.

Benefits and constraints. Over time STQM can improve customer satisfaction, reduce repeated rework and strengthen ownership. It needs sustained management support, meaningful measurements and time for teams to learn and change their processes.

Six Sigma

Six Sigma is a data-driven improvement methodology developed at Motorola to reduce variation and defects. Sigma (σ) denotes standard deviation. The name refers to a process whose nearest specification limit is six standard deviations from its centred mean. The conventional long-term benchmark is about 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), using the customary 1.5σ mean-shift convention.

DMAIC stageActivityBanking software example
DefineDescribe the customer problem, scope and critical-to-quality requirement (CTQ). Set an improvement objective.Reduce failed logins in comparable system-test batches.
MeasureDefine units, opportunities and defects. Check measurement consistency and establish the baseline.Collect login results and classify each failure by build and server instance.
AnalyseUse data and causal investigation to isolate major contributors.Pareto analysis highlights authentication configuration. A controlled test reproduces the failure on instances with stale settings.
ImprovePilot changes targeting verified causes and compare performance with the baseline.Automate configuration validation and test the corrected deployment.
ControlAssign ownership, monitoring, standard work and a response plan to sustain the gain.Apply deployment checks to every build and monitor login failure rates with a control chart.
DPMO = [defects ÷ (number of units × defect opportunities per unit)] × 1,000,000

Worked DPMO. Treat each transfer test as one unit and check three defined opportunities per unit for defects. In 2,000 units, record 12 failed checks. DPMO = 12 ÷ (2,000 × 3) × 1,000,000 = 2,000. If the same number of comparable opportunities later yields three defects, the result is 500 DPMO, a 75% decrease. One unit can contribute multiple defects if several checks fail.

Use and limitations. Six Sigma makes baseline and improvement comparisons measurable and can reduce recurring failure costs. It requires reliable data and consistent counting rules. DMAIC fits improvement of an existing process. PDCA can be used as a shorter iterative loop for implementing and checking changes.

10-mark structure. Define Six Sigma and explain σ and DPMO. Draw DMAIC, explain the goal and output of each step using one example, then show a DPMO calculation and state benefits and limitations.

Test Maturity Model (TMM)

The original Test Maturity Model was developed at the Illinois Institute of Technology in 1996. It provides a staged framework to assess and improve software testing. Software CMM assesses broader development process maturity. TMM focuses on how planned, integrated, measured and continuously improved testing is.

Two components. The maturity model defines five levels. The assessment model includes an assessment procedure, a questionnaire or instrument, and guidance for selecting and training assessors. Evidence comes from actual testing practices and records.

LevelCharacteristics and goalsTypical evidence
1 · InitialTesting is ad hoc, usually after coding, and mainly demonstrates that the product runs. No formal maturity goals are specified for this level.Informal execution and inconsistent records.
2 · Phase DefinitionSeparate testing from debugging. Define policy and goals, introduce test planning and basic test design techniques. Testing remains a planned phase after coding.Test policy, test plan and documented functional tests.
3 · IntegrationIntegrate testing into the software lifecycle. Establish a test organisation and training. Monitor the process and derive objectives early from requirements.Named roles, training records, requirement-based tests and lifecycle checkpoints.
4 · Management and MeasurementUse quantitative measurements for testing and product quality evaluation. Establish an organisation-wide review programme, record defects consistently and maintain reusable testware.Review records, test metrics, clear defect history and controlled test assets.
5 · Optimization, Defect Prevention and Quality ControlUse process data and root cause analysis to prevent recurring defects, control quality and continuously optimise testing.Prevention actions with owners, effectiveness trends and evidence of sustained improvement.

Structure within a level. Each level above Initial sets maturity goals. These are broken down into sub-goals implemented through activities, tasks and responsibilities (ATRs). Managers allocate resources, developers and testers implement and monitor practices, and clients contribute usage expectations and acceptance needs.

Assessment example. A team has test plans and basic scripted tests, but testing starts after coding and there is no testing training or lifecycle integration. The evidence is consistent with Phase Definition. To develop toward Integration, the team can assign clear test roles, bring testers into requirements reviews, introduce training and monitor the test process. Assess maturity from evidence across all relevant goals.

Benefits. TMM identifies process gaps, supplies a roadmap for improvement, and gives teams a way to compare progress over time. Use the original TMM level names in this syllabus. TMMi is a separate successor framework with different level names.

10-mark structure. Explain the need for TMM, name its maturity and assessment components, draw the five levels, describe the goals at each level and give one example of how a team advances.

Test yourself

What does test backlog growth mean when 32 defects arrive and 24 are closed?

BMI = 24 ÷ 32 × 100 = 75%. The backlog rises by 8 if there are no other status adjustments. Investigate arrival rate and fix capacity by severity before interpreting the product’s quality.

A control chart looks stable, but the mean failure rate is above the acceptance target. What should the team do?

Assess process capability against the acceptance target and improve the underlying process. Stability indicates predictable behaviour. The separate requirement still has to be met.

A team fixes a login defect after release and adds a deployment check. How should the costs be classified?

Emergency rework and retesting for the released defect are external failure costs. Designing a check that prevents the configuration error from recurring is a prevention investment. Use the purpose and timing of each activity to classify it.
