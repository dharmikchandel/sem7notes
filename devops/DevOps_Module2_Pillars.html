Collaboration, what makes teams smarter, and mentorship

CriticalAsked: Sep 2025, Sep 2023 (smarter teams)About 20 minSyllabus 2.1

This lesson gives you the definitions for the Sep 2025 question and the "smarter teams" idea for the Sep 2023 hackathon question. The second half of the 2025 question, how they build a growth mindset, is answered in lesson 2.

Collaboration

Collaboration is the intentional process of building toward a shared outcome through the interactions, input and support of multiple people. Examples:

  • Asynchronous code review.
  • Documentation and bug reports.
  • Weekly progress demos.
  • Pair programming.
  • Regular status updates.

What makes teams "smarter"

From the New York Times article "Why Some Teams Are Smarter Than Others", three things:

1. Communicationopen, frequent exchange of ideas and context2. Equal participationeveryone gets similar time to contribute3. Theory of mindknowing others hold a distinct perspective shaped by their context

Teams that score high on these, and that talk about non-work topics too, are more creative, more productive and build higher trust and empathy.

Mentorship

DirectionWhat it gives
Senior to juniorClassic mentoring. Builds junior skills through patient teaching.
Senior to seniorDeep knowledge sharing between peers.
Junior to seniorReinforces learning from everyone. A junior may know more than a senior on a given topic.
Junior to juniorPeers learn together on fast-growing teams.

Sponsors go further than mentors. A mentor advises. A sponsor actively advocates for the protégé's promotion.

Example: a senior pairs with a new hire on their first on-call shift, then later asks them to demo a tool the senior has not used.

Mindsets, learning organizations and the role of feedback

CriticalAsked: Sep 2025 (growth mindset), Sep 2024 (structural errors)About 25 minSyllabus 2.1

Mindsets are the personal beliefs we hold about ourselves and how we approach challenges. This lesson finishes the 2025 question, and the blameless culture here links back to the human-error question in Module 1.

Fixed mindset

  • Talent is innate and unchangeable.
  • Avoids challenges to escape failure.
  • Compares self to peers to prove worth.

"I'm just not a math person."

Growth mindset

  • Skills develop through effort and practice.
  • Treats failure as a learning opportunity.
  • Takes more risks and grows more.

"I haven't mastered this proof technique yet."

Six steps to cultivate a growth mindset

  1. Learn the fundamentals. The core skills your role and team need, not just industry experience.
  2. Develop your niche. Build knowledge in a gap area beyond your current responsibilities.
  3. Recognise strengths and progress. Assess your own performance honestly, independent of outside validation.
  4. Deliberate, quality practice. Keep challenging yourself so you do not coast as technology changes.
  5. Develop your working style. Find which tools, techniques and habits work best for you.
  6. Enhance the team style. Align personal preferences with team norms.

Mindsets and learning organizations

Blameful culture

  • Hunts for who "caused" the failure, to remove them.
  • Views mistakes as proof of inadequacy.
  • The organization stagnates and avoids risk.

Blameless culture

  • Assumes people and teams can improve.
  • Treats mistakes as learning opportunities.
  • Enables frequent feedback and communication.

The role of feedback

  • Praise effort, not traits. "You're so smart" (fixed-mindset praise) discourages future challenges. "You worked so hard" (growth-mindset praise) encourages future effort.
  • Frequency. Annual reviews delay problem-solving. Shorter feedback cycles make adjustments smaller and easier.
  • Ranking. Stack ranking ("rank and yank") labels the top 20% most productive and the middle 70% adequate, and the bottom 10% risk being cut. It pits employees against each other. GE retired it after finding it hurt collaboration more than it helped performance.

How collaboration and mentorship build a growth mindset

PracticeHow it grows the mindset
CollaborationCode review, pairing and demos give constant feedback and make asking for help normal. Open communication and equal participation mean mistakes are discussed, not hidden.
MentorshipMentors model learning from failure, give deliberate practice and honest feedback on effort. Junior to senior mentoring shows that anyone can teach, which breaks the idea that talent is fixed.
TogetherThey create a blameless, learning organization where skills are seen as developable, which is the growth mindset.

Conflict resolution styles

CriticalAsked: Sep 2025 (option)About 20 minSyllabus 2.1

There are five styles. The question has two parts: compare them, then pick the one that is least effective in DevOps and defend it.

StyleWhat it isExampleFit with DevOps
CompetitionPursue your own needs at others' expense. Win-lose. It ends the conflict only in the narrow sense that someone's needs win, decided by one side and not by mutual agreement.Insisting on your library choicePoor. Builds silos and rivalry.
AccommodationGive in to build goodwill.Deferring on a naming conventionFine for small things; your needs are lost if overused.
AvoidanceDodge the conflict. Breeds passive-aggression.Skipping the retro where it would come upWorst. Nothing is resolved or learned.
CompromiseEveryone gives up a little for a fair deal.Splitting the sprint across two prioritiesAcceptable when time is short.
CollaborationWin-win. Builds mutual understanding.Pairing to design an approach both teams needBest. Matches shared goals and trust.

Collaborative teams are more productive as a whole, and are viewed more favourably by their own members.

Which is least effective in DevOps?

Recommended answer: avoidance.

  1. It resolves nothing. The problem stays and grows.
  2. It breeds passive-aggression and hides issues, which destroys the transparency and trust DevOps depends on.
  3. DevOps relies on facing problems openly: retrospectives, blameless postmortems, feedback. Avoidance means skipping exactly those.
  4. Teams that avoid conflict also produce homogeneous thinking and less creativity.
Protect yourself in the exam

Your slides never state which style is "least effective". Competition is a strong alternative: win-lose, against shared ownership, the same spirit as stack ranking. Pick one, defend it in four lines, and add one sentence naming the other as the close runner-up. The marks come from tying your choice to DevOps values.

Affinity: what it is, tie strength and benefits

CriticalAsked: Oct 2022 (what is affinity), and it is the base of 3 more questionsAbout 20 minSyllabus 2.2

Affinity has been tested in four of your five papers. Lessons 5, 6 and 7 are one block: what it is (this lesson), what it needs (lesson 6), and how to measure it (lesson 7). Read them in order.

Work has five familiar categories

CategoryMeaningExample
ReactionaryResponding to somethingReplying to a Slack ping, emails, tickets
PlanningScheduling and prioritising other workSprint planning
ProceduralMaintenance workFollow-ups, expense reports
InsecurityChecking your own reputation and online statusChecking how a post was received
Problem solvingWork that needs creativity and focusDesigning a fix

The sixth category: relationship work

Relationship work is the social catalyst for all other work. It shortens the time to get other work done, reduces communication barriers between people, and builds trust based on regard for each other. A quick chat that unblocks a stuck ticket is relationship work in action.

What is affinity?

Definition

Affinity is the measure of relationship strength between individuals, teams, business units, even companies. It is incredibly hard to measure accurately. Example: how much Dev and Ops trust each other before an incident is a sign of affinity.

Interpersonal ties within a team

  • Too much conflict: not enough internal stability to reach goals.
  • Too much avoidance: homogeneous thought and less creativity.
  • Balance both. That is what makes a team resilient.

Four factors of a strong tie

1. Shared time

Working together strengthens relationships.

2. Intensity

Overcoming a crisis together, like an outage.

3. Shared stories

Opening up about wins and struggles.

4. Shared support

Covering on-call, helping with an issue.

Pairing through a two-week outage builds intensity faster than months of routine work.

Benefits of improved affinity

1. Shortened cycle time

  • Cycle time is work start to delivery.
  • Shorter cycles mean less wasted time.
  • Teams iterate faster. A trust-based code review gets features out in days, not weeks.

2. Fewer communication barriers

  • Miscommunication is a hidden cost.
  • Direct, honest conversation.
  • Less passive-aggression and less duplicated work.

3. Trust and innovation

  • Trust: less duplication, higher quality, better retention.
  • Innovation needs trust and blamelessness.
  • People take creative risks without fear.

Requirements for affinity

CriticalAsked: Oct 2022, Sep 2023About 20 minSyllabus 2.2

The most repeated Module 2 question: four requirements. Write all four, each with a definition and a one-line example, and you have the 10 marks.

Memory hook: S, E, S, C

Slack, Explicit values and goals, Space, Collaboration and cooperation.

Try first: say the four aloud with one detail each

Slack: a planned buffer, not idle time. Explicit values: relationship work must be stated and celebrated. Space: shared spots for ad hoc collaboration. Collaboration and cooperation: the culture rewards relationship-building.

1. Slack

  • Not idle time. It is an intentional buffer.
  • Prevents over-assigning work to people.
  • Absorbs unplanned or longer-than-expected work.
  • Example: a team sees 20 hours of unplanned work a week, so it plans for 20 or more hours of buffer.

2. Explicit values and goals

  • What is not planned for often does not happen.
  • Relationship work must be explicitly valued.
  • Managers should state the outcomes they want, then celebrate them.
  • Example: a skills matrix that scores mentoring and communication, not only code shipped.

3. Space

  • Shared spaces for ad hoc collaboration, never at the cost of individual workspace.
  • In-house coffee bars offer a mental break.
  • Helps teams gather information on their own.
  • Example: a shared coffee bar where Dev and Ops swap stories most mornings, unplanned.

4. Collaboration and cooperation

  • The culture must reward relationship-building work, not just tolerate it.
  • Actively promote it.
  • Avoid fostering a competitive environment.
  • Example: reward a pair-programmed fix instead of praising a solo "hero" save.

A team with plenty of slack but no shared space still struggles to build affinity, so all four are needed.

Answer sheet: what is affinity? Explain the requirements for affinity in detail 10 marks

Affinity and its requirements

Affinity is the measure of relationship strength between individuals, teams, business units and companies. It grows through relationship work, the sixth category of work. It shortens cycle time, reduces communication barriers and builds trust and innovation.

Four requirements:

  • Slack: an intentional buffer, not idle time. It stops over-assignment and absorbs unplanned work. E.g. plan 20 hours of buffer for 20 hours of weekly unplanned work.
  • Explicit values and goals: what is not planned often does not happen. Managers state and celebrate relationship work. E.g. a skills matrix that scores mentoring.
  • Space: shared spaces for ad hoc collaboration without taking away individual workspace. E.g. a coffee bar.
  • Collaboration and cooperation: the culture rewards relationship-building and avoids competition. E.g. reward pair programming over solo heroics.

All four are needed together.

Tools ecosystem: software development and monitoring

MajorNot asked on its own yetAbout 25 minSyllabus 2.3

Tools enhance culture. They do not replace it. There is no "DevOps in a box". The ecosystem has three pillars, and DevOps tools stress "we" over "me". Your tool choice becomes your team's common language. A hammer does not make you a carpenter; how you use it does. Two teams can both use Git, but one reviews every pull request and the other merges blindly: same tool, different culture.

Software developmentAutomation lesson 9Monitoring

Software development tools

Local development environment
A consistent setup lets new hires contribute from day one. Balance shared standards with personal choice. Too much customisation means "only Rahul can build this". Document it. Example: a shared Docker image, with your own editor theme.
Version control
Commit, compare, merge and restore any version. Git is the standard (GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket). Encourages forking, pull request review and shared rights.
Artifact management
An artifact is the output of a build step (.jar, Docker image). The repository stores, versions and controls access. Dev, test and prod all pull the same artifact, with no rebuilding. This removes "it works on my laptop".
TermMeaning
CommitA snapshot of changes saved to the repository history
ConflictTwo people edited the same line, so Git cannot decide. You compare and pick
Pull requestA request to review your code before it is merged into the main branch
Cherry pickApply one specific commit from another branch
Dependency managementManaging how much your project relies on external libraries and their versions
PinningLocking a library to one exact version, such as numpy==1.24.3, so an update cannot break you overnight
PromotionMoving an artifact closer to production after it passes tests (test, then staging, then prod)

Monitoring

Monitoring tracks the current state against the desired state of your system, using metrics (numbers over time) and logs (event records).

  • System level: is the server alive, CPU %, memory %, disk I/O.
  • Application level: request latency, error rate (5xx), queue depth, page load speed.
Metric typeMeaningExample
CounterAlways increasingTotal requests = 1,042,883
GaugeGoes up and downCurrent CPU = 72%
HistogramDistribution of values90% of requests under 200 ms
SummaryPre-calculated percentilesp99 latency = 450 ms

Typical stack: Prometheus (collection) with Grafana (visualisation). For logs, the ELK stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) or Loki with Grafana. Log levels, from least to most serious: DEBUG, INFO, WARN, ERROR, CRITICAL.

Alerting: five factors to weigh

Impact (how many users, how much revenue), urgency (two minutes or two days), who is notified, resources (is someone available), cost (worth waking an engineer at 3am?). Bad alerts cause fatigue. A good alert is "payment errors above 1% for 5 minutes". A bad one is "CPU above 50%".

Real case

HealthCare.gov launched in 2013 with no monitoring. Servers crashed and outages went unnoticed for hours. Engineers learned of them from news reports. The cost was about $600 million.

Event handlers can respond automatically to known conditions. Example: Nagios sees Apache down and restarts it, so no one is paged (self-healing).

Automation: build automation, infrastructure automation, deployment terms

CriticalAsked: Oct 2025 (types of build automation), Sep 2024 (infrastructure terminology)About 30 minSyllabus 2.3

Two asked questions are here. Learn the three kinds of build automation, and the infrastructure terms. The deployment and testing terms at the end are quick extras.

Three kinds of build automation

On-demand

Triggered by a human. You click "Run build" or type a command. Useful for one-off or exploratory builds.

Scheduled

Triggered by a clock. Runs at a fixed time, for example nightly at 2 am. Useful for batch jobs and nightly test suites.

Triggered

Triggered by an event. Fires on git push, a pull request or a file change. The heart of CI/CD.

Example of a triggered build: you push a bug fix to GitHub, GitHub Actions detects the push and runs the build, tests and Docker image build, with results in about three minutes.

Server installation and provisioning

Automates the operating system, packages, users and network configuration on machines. Without it you SSH into 50 servers one by one, which is error-prone. Tools: Kickstart, Cobbler, cloud-init, AWS CloudFormation. Cloud provisioning is faster and cheaper than hardware.

Infrastructure automation (infrastructure as code)

Provision infrastructure through code, not clicks. Treat infrastructure code like software: version it, test it, review it. Tools: Terraform (cloud), Ansible (configuration), Helm (Kubernetes).

Write Terraform code→terraform plan (preview)→terraform apply (execute)→Cloud infrastructure created→Destroy and recreate any time
Configuration drift
Servers slowly change away from the desired state through manual patches and one-off fixes, until no two are alike.
Snowflake server
A server so uniquely hand-configured that nobody else understands it. Fragile and irreplaceable.
MTBF
Mean time between failures. Higher means more reliable.
MTTR
Mean time to repair. IaC lowers it, because you re-run the script.
Availability
MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR). 99.99% ("four nines") is under an hour of downtime a year.

What infrastructure automation should provide: management of configuration drift; elimination of snowflake servers; versioned infrastructure code; minimised complexity. The result is repeatable, consistent, auditable and resilient infrastructure.

CanaryRoll out to about 5% of users first, monitor, then expand to 100%

Types of build automation

Build automation compiles, tests and packages code without manual steps. There are three kinds:

  • On-demand: started by a human, for one-off or exploratory builds.
  • Scheduled: started by a clock, such as a nightly build and test run.
  • Triggered: started by an event, such as a git push or pull request. This is the heart of CI/CD.

Example: a push to GitHub triggers build, tests and a Docker image build in minutes.

Tools as accelerators of culture: choosing, standardising and retiring tools

MajorNot asked yetAbout 20 minSyllabus 2.3

This part of unit 2.3 is about how an organization chooses tools. Read it once. The main message: a tool survives only if it solves a real, felt problem, and the people who will use it are part of the decision.

Right tools for real problems
Ask: does it save time, reduce errors, and will the team use it daily? "It looked cool at a conference" is not a reason. Bad: a 5-person startup runs 3 microservices on a full Kubernetes cluster. Good: on-call engineers asked for a tracing tool, and Jaeger now finds slow queries in seconds.
Embracing open source
Forces real collaboration with small, reviewable commits. Avoid "Not Invented Here" syndrome. Benefits: transparency (security researchers find bugs), community (features you would never build), no licence cost, customisable and no vendor lock-in. Netflix, Etsy and Google open-sourced tools.
Standardisation of tools
One authoritative tool per category, used by all teams. Improves communication, cuts onboarding time and licence cost. Five teams on five trackers makes handoffs a nightmare.
Consistent process for tool analysis
Six steps: identify the real problem; audit existing tools; evaluate candidates; pilot with one team; train and migrate all; retire the old tool. Confirm every required feature exists in the replacement and include a training and migration plan.
Exceptions to standardisation
Decide case by case with justification. Valid: regulatory mandate (PCI, HIPAA), technical impossibility (GPU tooling), legal isolation, vendor contract. Invalid: "we have always done it this way", "our team is special", "it has a learning curve", "we like our tool better".
Selecting a tool
Weigh product development (is it actively patched?), community health (PR response time, issue resolution, release cadence, forum activity, code of conduct) and in-house customisation (open source is easiest to read, fix and extend). Also feature availability, budget, interoperability and vendor lock-in.
Auditing and eliminating tools
Regular audits prevent tool sprawl. Ask users: what delights or frustrates you, what refuels or drains you, what should we stop doing, what should we start? Process: identify all tools, check usage, interview users, find overlaps, decide keep or retire, plan migration, execute.
Impact of tools on culture
Tools that cut friction make talking more likely. Moving from email to Slack can cut decisions from two days to ten minutes. Monitoring tools create an observability culture; code review tools create a quality culture.

Understanding scaling and planning for it

CriticalAsked: Sep 2024 (vampire and zombie projects); Oct 2025 (scaling for organizations)About 25 minSyllabus 2.4

Scaling has three parts in your slides. This lesson is the first: what "scale" means and how to plan for it. Lesson 12 is scaling for teams, lesson 13 is scaling for organizations.

Planning for scaling this lessonScaling for teams lesson 12Scaling for organizations lesson 13

"Scale" means different things

  • Expanding the customer base.
  • Growing revenue.
  • Expanding a project or team to meet demand.

Planning for scaling: six things to plan

1. Organizational structure
Cross-functional teams give high bandwidth on one product. Single-function teams give deeper specialisation. Do not reorganise just for the sake of it. Too flat an org is as harmful as too hierarchical. Example: one squad of a designer, two developers and an ops engineer.
2. Locality
Multiple locations expose communication gaps fast. Written communication matters more when people are distributed. Each site brings new IT and infrastructure needs. Uneven access creates "second-class" offices. Example: only headquarters has the fast internet link.
3. Team size
Too small: not enough person-hours or knowledge. Too large (9 to 10 and up): slow decisions and groupthink over dissent. Sweet spot: 5 to 7 people. Beyond that, split into a new team. Example: a team of 12 becomes two teams of 6.
4. Growth and decline pressures
Internal: natural hiring to grow, or voluntary downsizing when things slow. External: economics, competition or acquisition force the organization to change. How a company handles being acquired shapes its future.
5. Release cycles
Faster releases mean faster response to bugs and pressure. But "fail forward fast" is not right for every release. Ask: how easy is it to release, and how critical is this release? Example: a car's embedded software may need a costly recall, not a push update.
6. Complexity and change
Bigger, older organizations carry more existing constraints, in large enterprises and the public sector alike. Long-standing bureaucracy limits collaboration and affinity. Example: a government agency needs legal sign-off before adopting what a startup ships the same day.

Scaling for teams: hiring, retention, heroes

MajorNot asked on its own yetAbout 25 minSyllabus 2.4

Read this once. If the examiner asks about "scaling" in general, this is the middle layer of your answer. The main idea: teams scale on shared purpose, not on individual heroics.

Drucker

Growing a team

Hiring as scaling
Hiring only for stack experience is a narrow, short-term bet. Look at interpersonal and cultural fit. Strong learning and critical-thinking skills adapt furthest. Example: a developer with strong learning skills picks up a new stack within weeks.
The real cost of growth
New hires need ramp-up time. Existing staff spend time getting them up to speed. Communication overhead grows fast with headcount. Not every task splits cleanly. Example: adding two juniors mid-sprint slows the team before it speeds it.
Subcontracted work
Outsourcing IT or ops as "cost centers" is common. The dollar savings hide a cost: lost collaboration and affinity. Mixed in-house and outsourced teams can become a conflict source. Example: outsourced ops never invited to planning, so handoffs keep breaking.

Retention: four things together

Compensation

  • Feel paid fairly.
  • Staying nets about 3% (about 1% after inflation); switching often nets 10 to 30%.
  • Pay transparency and clear bands build trust.

Non-monetary

  • Remote work.
  • Flexible hours.
  • Education.
  • Paid leave, health insurance, daycare.

Growth

  • Lack of advancement is a top reason people leave.
  • People want more independence and trust over time.
  • Example: 25% of the week to explore a new area.

Workload and burnout

  • Challenging but doable.
  • Check in regularly.
  • Burnout is long-term exhaustion and lost interest.

Culture and culture fit has three layers: ideas (what the organization values), customs (how work, promotions and news are handled) and social behaviors (how people really talk and listen). Example: do seniors talk over juniors in meetings?

Hero culture is a problem

  • One hero is a single point of failure.
  • Heroics hide the real, unsustainable size of the workload.
  • What happens when that person leaves or burns out? Example: only one engineer knows the deploy process, and she is on vacation during an outage.

Three strategies to strengthen a team

1. Keep teams small and flexible

  • Large teams share knowledge less.
  • Tasks locked to individuals create bottlenecks.
  • Watch for single points of knowledge, like the one person who knows billing.

2. Foster collaboration

  • Positive interdependence.
  • Effective communication.
  • Individual and group accountability.

3. Manage conflict

  • Unaddressed conflict hurts the whole organization.
  • Building relationships turns competition into cooperation.
  • Talk directly instead of routing through managers.

What "smarter teams" means for judging

The phrase comes from lesson 1: the New York Times article "Why Some Teams Are Smarter Than Others". A smarter team is not the team with the star coder. It has three traits: communication, equal participation and theory of mind. So the judge should watch how the whole team behaves, not only the demo. One way: ask each member a question and see whether everyone can answer.

Selecting the three winners

  1. Score. Give each of the 20 teams 0 to 5 on each of the four pillars, plus the culture foundation.
  2. Verify. Do not trust the demo alone. Ask different members questions and read the commit history (trust but verify).
  3. Rank. Total the scores and take the top three.
  4. Break ties on outcome. Prefer the team whose solution delivers a real result for users over the one with more features (outcome over output, from Module 1).

Answer sheet: pillars of DevOps to pick 3 winners 10 marks

Judging the hackathon with the pillars of DevOps

DevOps rests on a culture of shared ownership, generative and blameless, where outcomes matter more than outputs. On top of that culture stand four pillars, which I would use as my judging criteria.

1. Collaboration, mindset and trust. Smarter teams are not teams with one star. They have communication, equal participation and theory of mind. I would watch whether all members contribute, whether they treat failure as learning (growth mindset), and whether they handle conflict openly.

2. Affinity. The strength of relationships. I would look for smooth handoffs, little rework, and quick recovery from a blocker. These show shorter cycle time and fewer communication barriers.

3. Tools and automation. A shared repository with reviews, an automated build and test pipeline triggered on each push, a deployed running solution, and monitoring.

4. Scaling. A right-sized team (5 to 7), shared purpose and no hero. Another member should be able to present the work, so there is no single point of knowledge.

Method. Score every team 0 to 5 per pillar, verify by questioning different members and reading commit history, rank by total, and break ties by the team whose solution delivers the better real outcome.

The three highest-scoring teams win, because they show the behaviours that make teams smarter, not just clever code.
